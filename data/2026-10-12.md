Abstract: Ising machines solve combinatorial optimization problems by annealing a network of spins. On dense problems, the least expensive hardware step updates all spins at once: stochastic cellular automata (SCA) decide every spin in parallel and read couplings only for the spins that flip. Such synchronous updates create an echo: the previous state of each spin returns to it through its neighbors. On K2000, a Max-Cut benchmark on a 2,000-node complete graph, two-thirds of all flips undo the same spin's flip one step earlier. This paper presents Anechoic, an FPGA Ising machine that subtracts this echo from every decision. Dynamical mean-field theory identifies the echo as an Onsager reaction; its one-step strength is the number of spins whose flip probability is strictly between 0 and 1, divided by twice the temperature. The decision logic already flags these spins, so the correction costs one population count per step; a temperature-scaled form needs none. An RTL engine executes a dense 2,000-spin step in 18.1 cycles plus 0.14 per flip, so every saved step and flip saves time. Both forms find better K2000 cuts at every step budget than five published binary-spin algorithms at their papers' settings. Twelve engines at 250 MHz on an AMD Alveo V80 reach a cut of at least 33,000 with 99% probability in 0.05 ms, 5.2 times faster than the fastest published hardware, at 5.4 mJ per solution; with 2-bit couplings, the temperature-scaled form is also faster than uncorrected SCA on 49 of 51 G-set graphs. On an NVIDIA GH200 GPU, the same engine is 2.3 times slower and needs 8.0 times more energy per solution, though its 240 concurrent anneals give 2.3 times the throughput. As a GlobalFoundries 22 nm ASIC at 1.25 GHz, twelve engines would reach the target in about 10 microseconds, 26 times faster than the fastest published hardware. The code is available at https://github.com/skethz/anechoic.

Abstract: Deep-space communications operate under intermittent connectivity, long propagation delays, and relay storage constraints. In such networks, the Bundle Protocol enables store-carry-forward communication, requiring relay nodes to manage limited storage and scarce contact opportunities. Relay-side scheduling and dropping decisions are inherently goal-oriented: transmission resources should be allocated to bundles most valuable to downstream mission objectives. Conventional schedulers, such as FIFO, do not explicitly optimize task utility and may provide unfair service across heterogeneous planetary sources. This paper presents a closed-loop modeling and learning framework for relay-side bundle and flow management in a contact-driven deep-space network, where lunar and Martian updates are forwarded through a shared-buffer relay to Earth. We quantify destination-side information freshness using excess age $Δ_i^e(t)=[Δ_i(t)-d_i(t)]^+$, where $Δ_i(t)$ denotes the Age of Information of source $i$ and $d_i(t)$ is a source-dependent delay baseline. Based on this metric, we define a goal-oriented loss that supports source-dependent nonlinear penalties, allowing prioritization according to mission relevance. We formulate relay control as a constrained semi-Markov decision process (CSMDP) and solve it using masked Lagrangian Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Feasibility-aware action masking restricts transmissions to bundles that can complete within the remaining contact window. DTNSim simulations indicate improved age-based utility and inter-flow fairness compared with baseline schedulers. The proposed framework jointly optimizes relay-side bundle scheduling, dropping, and flow control under contact-driven dynamics with explicit fairness considerations across heterogeneous sources.

Abstract: The increasing demand for GPU-accelerated machine learning inference has exposed limitations in centralized GPU infrastructures, including high cost, limited elasticity, and long wait times under resource constraints. At the same time, modern Apple Silicon-based macOS devices are equipped with powerful GPUs that remain largely idle during normal usage. This paper presents Minions, a dynamic and distributed virtual GPU cloud that aggregates idle Apple Silicon GPUs across macOS devices to execute large-scale machine learning inference workloads. Minions enables teams to leverage existing hardware without requiring additional GPU procurement. The system provides flexible GPU inference task execution, fault tolerance, and efficient resource utilization. Our experience shows that idle macOS devices can be transformed into a practical distributed GPU inference platform in environments where centralized GPU resources are constrained.

Abstract: Agentic workloads resend long contexts that barely change between calls. Prefix caching now skips most of their prefill, yet every request must still be tokenized in full before the cache can even be consulted. Tokenization, a CPU stage long treated as negligible, becomes the largest part of time-to-first-token (TTFT), and a frontend that tokenizes for a whole GPU fleet limits how many GPUs it can feed. We present GraphTok, a GPU tokenizer that runs the entire encoding pipeline, from pre-tokenization through byte-pair encoding (BPE) merging to token-ID assembly, as one replayable CUDA graph. GraphTok reproduces the reference token IDs bit-for-bit on GPT-2, Llama 3, Qwen 2.5, DeepSeek-V3, and Gemma 3, hands short inputs to a CPU engine where the GPU cannot win, and plugs into unmodified vLLM and NVIDIA Dynamo. GraphTok encodes one million GPT-2 tokens in 1.7-3.2 ms including data transfers, and on Llama 3 it is 4.7-6.0x faster than gigatoken, the strongest CPU tokenizer. On TokTier's own single-request benchmark, it is 2.6-3.9x faster than gigatoken and up to 18.1x faster than TokTier. In vLLM with a cached prefix, it lowers mean TTFT by 2.1-2.4x. The largest gain is capacity. Within a 2 s P99 target, one Dynamo frontend with GraphTok sustains 518 requests/s of 121k-token traffic, the busiest-hour load of 56 GPUs, against 296 with gigatoken and 18.5 with the default tokenizer. Across 121k to one million tokens, this is 1.75-2.5x the load gigatoken sustains, with a P99 up to 64x lower where gigatoken's tail breaks.
